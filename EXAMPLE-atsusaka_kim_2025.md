# Measurement Errors in Ranking Questions

**Full citation:** Atsusaka, Yuki, and Seo-young Silvia Kim. 2025. "Addressing Measurement Errors in Ranking Questions for the Social Sciences." *Political Analysis* 33(4): 339–360. doi:10.1017/pan.2024.33.

*This is the instructor's worked example.*

---

## 1. What the paper establishes

Survey respondents who rank a set of items sometimes produce rankings that are arbitrary—that is, unrelated to any underlying preference. The paper calls these random responses and shows that they can bias ranking-based quantities of interest. The field's default remedy of randomizing item order does not remove the bias; it only makes the bias predictable, pulling observed rankings toward a uniform distribution.

The paper's fix is design-based: pair the target ranking question with an "anchor" ranking question whose correct answer is known in advance, use it to estimate what share of responses are random, and correct the estimates. In the authors' own survey of the United States, an estimated 31.6% of responses were random.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether a paper analyzing ranking survey data acknowledges random or meaningless responses at all.** Most do not.

**(b) Check whether the paper randomizes item order and, if so, treats that as sufficient.** This is the specific, common, and consequential error. A paper that randomizes item order and says nothing further has not addressed measurement error; it has made the random responses uniformly distributed.

**(c) For population-level summaries under item order randomization, check the direction of the residual bias against the paper's claim.** Random responses pull marginal quantities such as average ranks and first-choice shares toward their values under indifference. A null or flat ranking may therefore mask underlying differences, while strong rank differences are conservative with respect to this specific contamination mechanism. This shortcut does not extend generally to regression coefficients or covariate-specific predictions, whose bias can depend on the target ranking profile, predictor, and values at which other covariates are fixed.

**(d) Check whether the paper distinguishes detection from estimation and, if it uses an anchor question, supports the assumptions needed for correction.** A uniformity test can detect evidence of random responses but cannot estimate their prevalence. Estimating the random-response share requires a randomized anchor with an ex-ante known answer and a defensible assumption that the population proportion of random responses is constant across the anchor and target questions. The reviewer should check whether the corrected conclusions are reported.

**(e) If an already-completed survey used item order randomization but did not include an anchor question, check whether the conclusions are sensitive to plausible random-response rates.** Attention-check failure rates and estimates from similar measures can serve as benchmarks for a sensitivity range. This is a second-best analysis for data that have already been collected, not a replacement for including an anchor in a new survey. The benchmark should not be described as an identified estimate of the random-response share unless the paper supplies an additional argument linking it to the target ranking question. A benchmark from a check placed next to the target question and in a ranking format is stronger than one from a distant screener. The upper end of the range deserves the most scrutiny because overestimating the random-response share can over-correct the bias and inflate Type I error.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** The authors' own literature audit: "only 3 out of 28 studies that use ranking questions published in the American Political Science Review, the American Journal of Political Science, and the Journal of Politics, 2012–2023, mention potential measurement errors" (p. 1). Twenty-five of twenty-eight published papers in the discipline's top three journals are silent on this.

**For (b).** "[R]andomization alone does not remove the bias. Instead, randomization makes random responses follow a uniform distribution" (p. 2). The formal version appears at Equation 7 (p. 8), which decomposes observed rankings into a non-random component and a uniform component weighted by the random-response rate. Randomization pins down the *shape* of the contamination; it does not remove it.

Footnote 2 on p. 2 documents four published studies that randomized the presentation order of items in ranking questions. For example, Malhotra and Margalit (2014, 1002) write, "[t]he order in which the six traits were presented was fully randomized to ensure that primacy effects did not bias the rankings." The footnote establishes only that these studies randomized item order; whether they otherwise address random-response measurement error must be verified in each study and its supporting materials.

**For (c).** "[E]ven under randomization, random responses can mask otherwise salient ranked preferences among respondents and 'dilute' empirical results" (p. 2). Restated formally on p. 8: random responses "pull any estimates towards what researchers may observe when all respondents are indifferent among available items." This gives a direction for population-level summaries of the rank distribution. It does not license a generic directional claim for covariate models: on p. 17, the authors explain that the bias also depends on the target ranking profile, target independent variable, and reference values at which other variables are fixed.

**For (d).** The anchor-free diagnostic is a uniformity test on recorded responses, where "non-uniformity in the test suggests the presence of random responses" (p. 11). It shows that contamination exists, not how much. Estimating the share requires an anchor question, "an auxiliary ranking question whose 'correct answer' is ex-ante known to researchers" (p. 8), and "[t]he item order in the anchor question must be randomized just as it must be randomized for the target question" (p. 8). The method also assumes that "[t]he proportion of random responses remains constant across the target and anchor questions" (p. 9). The paper recommends making that assumption more plausible by giving the questions similar substantive topics, instruction length, number and length of items, and locations in the survey (p. 9), and it also requires question order randomization (p. 8, n. 10). The authors' application finds 31.6% random responses in their anchor question (p. 13), and they conclude that "more than 30% of respondents can fall prey to random responses, and not accounting for this can affect our substantive conclusions" (p. 20).

**For (e).** The authors benchmark their own estimate this way. Having estimated that 31.6% of responses are random, they write, "We believe that this is not an unreasonably high (or low) estimate," and compare it with attention-check failure rates of 34% to 41% and with similar estimates from ranking and conjoint studies (p. 14). They use these figures to judge plausibility, not to estimate, and the paper says what makes a substitute measure credible: any anchor or manipulation check should be "situated adjacent to the main ranking question of interest, and preferably also a ranking format" (p. 18). It also says which way to err: "we recommend underestimating rather than overestimating the proportion of random responses in the target question because it leads to under-correction of the bias, which guards against inflating Type I errors" (p. 14). Because Equation 7 (p. 8) links observed rankings to the random-response rate, a reviewer can ask for corrected estimates across a benchmark range, treating the upper end cautiously because it risks over-correction. Such benchmarks do not become identified estimates of the random-response rate, and new surveys should still use a properly designed anchor. If a paper instead drops respondents who fail an attention check, it assumes they are exactly the random responders in the target question; the authors call this "much stronger" than assuming a constant share, and find that two attention checks correlate at only 0.25 (p. 17).

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** survey research where respondents produce a *full* ranking of J items, and where a substantive claim rests on the resulting ranks: average ranks, the ranked ordering itself, the proportion placing an item first, or a regression with a rank-derived outcome. Checks (a) and (b) apply to any such paper. Check (c) applies only when item order was randomized; without randomization, the direction of measurement error is unpredictable. Its simple attenuation claim applies to population-level rank summaries, not generally to regression coefficients or covariate-specific predictions. The diagnostic part of (d) applies when item order was randomized and the recorded response patterns are available; its estimation and correction requirements apply when the design included an anchor question. Check (e) is a second-best sensitivity-analysis fallback for already-collected data that used item order randomization but included no anchor.

**Does not directly apply to:** rating scales, feeling thermometers, single-choice or multinomial items, or check-all-that-apply batteries. The paper's formal results are derived for full rankings. It notes that uniformity tests, randomization, and anchors may extend to binary, multinomial, and ordered-choice questions, but leaves those non-ranking applications and their distinct challenges to future work (p. 20).

**Does not apply to:** partial rankings, top-k rankings, or rankings with ties. The authors are explicit that "our current framework focuses on full-ranking questions" and list these as extensions for future research (p. 20). This is the sharpest boundary in the paper, and a check that triggers on top-3 designs overreaches past what the authors proved.

**Does not apply to** standard forced-choice conjoint designs, where respondents choose between paired profiles rather than order J items.

**Partial application:** ranked-choice voting ballot data. The paper uses one paragraph on RCV ballot errors as motivation (p. 2) and later says it hopes the framework will inform election administrators (p. 20), but the estimators are developed for survey settings with a randomizable anchor. Flag the concern; do not assert that the correction applies.

## 5. What failure looks like

A paper reports an uncorrected quantity derived from a full-ranking question and treats randomized item order as its only safeguard against random responses. It reports neither the uniformity diagnostic, which can detect evidence of random responses but cannot estimate their prevalence, nor a randomized and substantively comparable anchor question, which is needed to estimate the random-response share and apply the proposed correction. If no anchor was fielded, the paper also offers no sensitivity analysis using a plausible benchmark, such as the failure rate on a nearby attention check or an estimate from a similar measure. If the observed ranks are flat, a reviewer should note that random-response contamination could have diluted the differences, not infer that contamination caused the null result.

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** Malhotra, Neil, and Yotam Margalit. 2014. "Expectation Setting and Retrospective Voting." *The Journal of Politics* 76(4): 1000–1016. Respondents rank all six displayed candidate traits from most to least positive, and the authors use the resulting average ranks as evidence that voters value optimism as a leadership quality (p. 1002). The main article reports randomizing trait order to prevent primacy effects but does not report a diagnostic or correction for random responding in this ranking. Because a substantive claim rests directly on the complete ranking, the case is within scope: checks (a)–(d) should run, with (c) implying that random responses would attenuate the reported rank differences. Because the survey was already run and the article reports no anchor, (e) supplies the appropriate second-best sensitivity analysis. A reviewer should still inspect the supporting materials before concluding that the study took no further response-quality measures.

**Should NOT trigger:** Hainmueller, Jens, Daniel J. Hopkins, and Teppei Yamamoto. 2014. "Causal Inference in Conjoint Analysis: Understanding Multidimensional Choices via Stated Preference Experiments." *Political Analysis* 22(1): 1–30. A near-miss: it elicits multidimensional preferences and explicitly arranges profile attributes to ease respondents' cognitive burden and limit primacy and recency effects (p. 4), so a careless agent may want to trigger. But respondents choose between paired profiles or rate individual profiles rather than producing a full ranking of J items. Atsusaka and Kim's ranking-specific results therefore do not directly apply. Triggering here is the failure mode that makes a review agent unusable.
