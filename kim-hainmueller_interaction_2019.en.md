## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Some manuscripts make a substantive claim about an interaction with at least one continuous variable. Check whether such a manuscript treats following Brambor, Clark, and Golder (2006) as enough protection.** Their advice has three steps. Include all constitutive terms. Do not read the coefficients on those terms as unconditional effects. Plot marginal effects with confidence intervals from the linear model. These steps alone cannot reveal the two problems this paper studies (p. 164). The first problem is a violation of the linear interaction effect (LIE) assumption. This assumption says that the effect of the treatment changes with the moderator at one constant rate. The second problem is a lack of common support: at some moderator values where effects are reported, there are too few observations, or the treatment does not vary (pp. 164–165). For such a manuscript, ask for a raw-data plot (p. 168). It plots the outcome against the moderator for each treatment group. It adds a linear fit and a LOESS curve (a flexible local fit) on top.

**(b) For each range of the moderator where marginal effects are reported, check whether there are enough observations and whether the treatment actually varies in that range.** Check whether the plot shows the distribution of the moderator below it. If the treatment is binary, check whether this distribution is shown separately for the treated and control groups. If it is not, ask for it. A distribution that combines both groups makes the second condition hard to judge (p. 172, n. 19). In a range without support, the estimated effect comes from the assumed functional form, not from the data (p. 167; p. 178). So ask the authors to limit their claims to the range that has support. You may ask whether the result changes when a few extreme observations are dropped. But treat this only as a test of fragility, not as a fix (p. 181, n. 28).

**(c) Check whether the manuscript reports a flexible estimate next to the linear marginal-effect line. Examples are binning estimates by terciles (thirds) of the moderator, or a kernel estimate. If the manuscript has one, read two things.** First, if the bin estimates are far from the line or are non-monotonic (going up and then down, or the reverse), read this as evidence that the LIE assumption fails (p. 173). Second, look at how the manuscript supports the claim ‘the effect changes with the moderator’. Check whether it tests the difference between the effects at low and high moderator values. One example is a test at the medians of the lowest and highest terciles. A significant interaction coefficient alone is not this test (p. 183). If both the treatment and the moderator are continuous, ask for the raw-data plots in both directions (p. 187). One plot shows the effect of the treatment given the moderator. The other shows the effect of the moderator given the treatment.

**(d) Look at the form of the conclusion and check which way it could be wrong.** Some conclusions are about the effect at specific moderator values. Examples are a threshold above which the effect becomes significant, or the size of the effect at an extreme value. For such a conclusion, check how many observations lie near those values and whether the treatment varies there. This memo's interpretation is that these claims are the first to lose significance when the functional-form assumption is relaxed. The error can also go the other way. Some manuscripts conclude that the effect does not change with the moderator, because the linear interaction term is not significant. In that case, check whether the manuscript reports a flexible estimate that could find a non-monotonic pattern. A weak linear interaction can hide a strong conditional effect (p. 173). Here the reviewer should only point out the possibility and should not claim that a hidden effect exists.

**(e) Some manuscripts use this paper's diagnostics. Check whether such a manuscript treats a failure to reject linearity in the Wald test as evidence that the LIE assumption holds (p. 183).** This matters most when the sample is small, when the bins have few observations, or when the moderator is skewed. For such a manuscript, also ask for a plot that shows whether the bin estimates actually fall on the linear line.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** The authors list the three recommendations of Brambor, Clark, and Golder (2006) and say that they are "nowadays often considered the best practice in political science" (p. 164). Right after this, they name what these guidelines miss: "two important problems that are currently often overlooked and not detected by scholars using the existing guidelines" (p. 164). This sentence directly supports the check: following these guidelines tells us nothing about the two problems. For the LIE problem, the same page adds that many conclusions "rest on a modeling artifact that goes undetected even when applying the current best practice guidelines" (p. 164). For common support, the replication of Chapman (2009) says: "The common practice of fitting the standard multiplicative interaction model and computing the conditional marginal effects from this model will not alert the researcher to this problem." (p. 178). There, ‘this problem’ is the lack of common support. So this sentence should not be used as evidence about the LIE problem. The diagnostic to ask for also comes from the paper. The first diagnostic the authors recommend is a scatterplot of the raw data (p. 168). The first item of their revised checklist also asks researchers to use this plot to check whether a linear fit describes the conditional relationships well (p. 187).

**For (b).** In their revised checklist, the authors advise: "Researchers should avoid computing marginal effects in areas where the data is too sparse either because there are no observations for those values of the moderator or there is no variation in the treatment." (p. 187). The sentence names two separate conditions. So a reviewer can compare the horizontal axis of the plot with the moderator's distribution and judge each one. The reason to ask for the distribution by group is in a footnote. Berry, Golder, and Milton (2012) advise adding the moderator's distribution to the plot, and the authors go one step further: "in the case of a binary treatment it is advantageous to distinguish in the histogram between the two groups to get a better sense of the overlap across groups" (p. 172, n. 19). The replications show the second condition failing in practice. In Chapman (2009), all observations with a U.N. authorization were in the lowest tercile of the moderator (p. 178). In Clark and Golder (2006), 59% of the moderator values were zero, and the treatment did not vary at zero. So the effect there was not identified (p. 182). The paper also guides the reviewer's wording. If the model happens to be correct, estimates in a range without support are still consistent and unbiased (p. 167, n. 11). So a reviewer should not call these estimates biased. It is more accurate to say that they rely on the assumed functional form and are "model dependent and fragile" (p. 178). The request to limit claims to the supported range comes from the last item of the revised checklist: "marginal effects should only be computed for areas with sufficient common support" (p. 187). On extreme observations: in Malesky, Schuler, and Tran (2012), dropping four extreme leverage points made the effect estimates flat. These four points were less than 0.9% of the observations (p. 181). But the authors state: "we have no theoretical rationale to drop these data points. This is merely a robustness check" (p. 181, n. 28). So check (b) asks for this only as a diagnostic, not as a fix.

**For (c).** The revised checklist sets a condition for using the linear model: "The standard linear interaction model and marginal-effect plots should only be used if the estimates from the binning and/or kernel estimator suggest that the interaction is really linear" (p. 187). So a manuscript with no flexible estimate has not shown that it meets this condition. The authors also give a rule for reading the plot: "When the estimates from the binning estimator are far off the line or when they are non-monotonic, we have evidence that the LIE assumption does not hold." (p. 173). The second question comes from the first criterion the authors used to score their replications. They test whether the effect at the median of the lowest tercile differs from the effect at the median of the highest tercile (p. 183). They explain: "This criterion provides a formal test of the extent to which the data contains evidence of a significant interaction effect once we relax the stringent LIE assumption" (p. 183). This test matters in practice. The authors relaxed the LIE assumption in their replications. In about 70% of the cases, they then could not reject the null that the effect is the same at typical low and typical high values (p. 164). This number only shows how common the problem is. So the direct evidence for (c) is the definition on p. 183. The request for plots in both directions comes from the authors' advice to draw the raw-data plots both ways. The reason is that the standard model assumes linearity in both directions (p. 187).

**For (d).** The first question comes from the authors' criticism of current practice. They say that many researchers treat the linear model as the true model, go beyond average conclusions, and "instead claim to have estimated the marginal effect of the treatment at specific values of the moderator, interpretations which rely heavily on the linear functional form being correct" (p. 165; the sentence begins on p. 164). This quote names both the kind of sentence a reviewer should look for and what such a claim depends on. The replication of Malesky, Schuler, and Tran (2012) gives a concrete case: "there are very few observations which exhibit levels of Internet penetration higher than about 2.5, which is the point above which the effect of the transparency intervention starts to become significant according to the original model" (p. 181). With a flexible estimator, confidence intervals become wider where support is thin. The authors call this "desirable because it makes clear the increasing lack of common support" (p. 175). The Chapman replication shows the same pattern (p. 178). Check (d) says that claims about specific values tend to lose significance. This direction is this memo's interpretation of these passages. The authors do not state it as a general rule. The second question rests on a simulation. The authors use data in which the true marginal effect is quadratic (p. 168). They find that "the standard linear model indicates a slightly negative, but overall very weak, interaction effect, whereas the binning estimates reveal that the effect of D is actually strongly conditioned by X" (p. 173). They note that the effect may be "nonlinear or nonmonotonic" (p. 166). But this evidence is a single simulation. Also, the replication sample included only studies that claimed an interaction (p. 175). So the paper cannot tell us how often a non-significant linear interaction hides real differences in actual manuscripts. That is why (d) stops at pointing out the possibility.

**For (e).** The authors rewrite the binning model so that it contains the linear model as a special case. They then use a Wald test of the null that the eight added parameters are all zero (p. 183). They warn that failing to reject this null "does not necessarily mean that the LIE assumption holds, especially when the sample size is small and the test is underpowered", and so they call their own coding "lenient" (p. 183). Some manuscripts use the same test and present a failure to reject as proof of linearity. These manuscripts make the inference that the authors warn against.

## 4. Scope

**Scope shared by all checks.** The five checks apply only when a manuscript meets three conditions. First, at least one of the treatment and the moderator is continuous. Second, a substantive claim rests on the interaction, that is, on the claim that the treatment effect changes with the moderator. Third, this effect is estimated with a multiplicative interaction term in OLS or in a linear fixed-effects model. These conditions match the case where the authors say the existing guidelines fall short (p. 164). They also match the rules the authors used to select their replication sample (p. 175). Which variable is called the treatment and which the moderator is arbitrary (p. 166, n. 7). So read the continuous one as the moderator. Within this scope, each check has its own narrower conditions.

**(a).** Check (a) applies when the only support for the interaction claim is the constitutive terms and a linear marginal-effect plot. It stays silent when the manuscript already reports a raw-data plot or a flexible estimate, including separate estimates for each tercile. Then (b) and (c) read those results. It also stays silent when the interaction term is only a control or appears in one column of a robustness table, with no substantive claim built on it.

**(b).** Check (b) applies when two things are true. First, marginal effects are reported over a wide range of the moderator, often from its minimum to its maximum. Second, the moderator is skewed, or the treatment is linked to the moderator, so some ranges may have almost no units from one of the groups. Among the replications, Chapman (2009) and Clark and Golder (2006) were such cases (p. 178; p. 182). The check stays silent when the manuscript plots marginal effects only within the supported range and shows the distribution by group. Do not use an L-kurtosis above 0.16 as the trigger. That cutoff is the coding rule the authors used for their replications, not a general standard (p. 183, n. 33). Partial application (this memo's interpretation): suppose the treatment is randomized independently of the moderator. Then the design mostly ensures that the treatment varies, so check only for thin tails. Do not skip that tail check, though. The authors' example of a case where the assumptions hold is Huddy, Mason, and Aarøe (2015). It was a survey experiment. Even so, sufficient support covered only about 0.3 to 1 on the moderator's 0-to-1 scale (p. 176).

**(c).** Check (c) applies when three things are true. The moderator is continuous, or it is ordinal with many values but entered as a linear term. The conditional effect is estimated with a single linear interaction model. The claim is about the direction or size of the change in the effect. The first question (linearity) stays silent when the manuscript already uses a flexible method to estimate how the effect varies. Examples are separate estimates by range, a GAM, a kernel method, or machine learning methods. Binning relaxes the LIE assumption by fitting the interaction terms separately in each bin (p. 171). The authors say they do not intend to criticize other flexible methods (p. 163, n. 1), and they list machine learning methods as another option (p. 187). Even then, the second question still asks whether those results actually show a difference between low and high values. The second question stays silent when the claim is not ‘the effect changes with the moderator’ but something like ‘the effect is positive at every level’. Partial application: when the sample is small and each bin has few observations, do not read noisy bin estimates as proof that the LIE assumption fails. Flexible estimators are less efficient than the linear model when the LIE assumption is true, and more bins need more observations (p. 170). In this case, ask for the diagnostic, but do not decide yet.

**(d).** The first question applies when the abstract or conclusion describes the effect at specific moderator values or ranges. Those values must also be in the tail of the distribution, or in a range with little variation in the treatment. The second question applies when the conclusion rejects a conditional hypothesis, or says the effect is the same everywhere, based on a non-significant linear interaction term. It applies only if the manuscript has no flexible estimate. The first question stays silent when the claim is only about an average slope, such as ‘in general, the effect is larger when the moderator is larger’. The authors accept that a regression can serve as "a linear approximation for the unknown true model" (p. 164). Such an average claim is checked instead by the low-versus-high test in (c). The second question stays silent when the manuscript only reports the non-significant result and does not conclude that the effect is the same everywhere. Partial application: flexible estimators are less efficient than the linear model (p. 170). So in a small sample, do not turn the second question into a claim that hidden differences exist.

**(e).** Check (e) applies when the manuscript uses this paper's diagnostics and keeps the linear model because the linearity test did not reject it. It applies only if, in addition, the sample is small, the bins have few observations, or the moderator is skewed. It stays silent for a manuscript that does not use the diagnostics; that case belongs to (a) and (c). It also stays silent for a manuscript whose test rejects linearity; that case belongs to (c). It also stays silent when the manuscript shows in a plot that the bin estimates fall on the linear line, because then the test is not the only evidence. In this memo's interpretation, the warning carries less weight when the sample is large. The authors' words are "especially when the sample size is small" (p. 183). So if bin estimates with narrow confidence intervals fall on the line in a large sample, the concern is weaker. Partial application: the warning is about the authors' own Wald test (p. 183). If the same logic is applied to another linearity test, such as adding a squared term, say that this is an analogy.

**Nearby cases where all five checks stay silent.** Sometimes both the treatment and the moderator are binary or have only a few categories. For this case, the authors recommend a fully saturated model, with a dummy for each category and all their interactions (p. 166). A saturated model makes no LIE assumption. So all five checks stay silent for designs such as a 2×2 factorial experiment. They also stay silent for mediation analysis, because the authors separate moderators from mediators (p. 166, n. 6). They stay silent for regressions without an interaction. The authors say that misspecification and lack of common support also affect regressions without interaction terms (p. 165). But the checks in this memo are about estimating and reading conditional marginal effects.

**Boundaries from the authors' stated limits.** The sharpest boundary is in the conclusion. The authors write that following their revised guidelines "does not guarantee that the model will be correctly specified" (p. 187). They also write that the checks "cannot help with other common problems such as endogeneity or omitted variables" (p. 188). So these checks should not be used to question whether the moderator is exogenous or whether the interaction has a causal meaning. In the other direction, a manuscript that passes all five checks should not be read as having a correct model or an identified effect. The authors also assume that the control variables are correctly specified (p. 166). So the functional form of the controls is outside this memo.

**Partial application: nonlinear models and triple interactions.** The replication sample included only OLS and linear fixed-effects models. The authors left out triple interactions because those models need "even more demanding assumptions" (p. 175). This covers interactions in nonlinear models such as logit or probit, and triple interactions. For these, raise the concern. But do not assume that this paper's diagnostics and estimators apply as they are.

## 5. What failure looks like

The manuscript draws a substantive conclusion from an interaction with a continuous moderator. In the methods section, the only protection it names is that it follows Brambor, Clark, and Golder (2006) and includes all constitutive terms. The evidence is one marginal-effect plot from the linear interaction model. The marginal-effect line in the plot is a straight line from the minimum to the maximum of the moderator. This straight shape is set by the model, not by the data (Equation 2, p. 166). Below the plot, there is no distribution of the moderator, or only a rug plot that combines the treated and control groups. The conclusion is about the effect at specific values. For example, it says that the effect becomes significant only above some threshold, or that the effect is largest where the moderator is largest. But neither the text nor the appendix says how many observations lie in that range, or whether both treated and control units are there. There is also no raw-data plot by treatment group, no tercile binning estimate, no kernel estimate, no test of the low-versus-high difference, and no result without the extreme observations. The authors' replication of Malesky, Schuler, and Tran (2012) shows this problem in numbers. About 80% of the moderator's density lay in 11% of the plotted range (p. 183, n. 33). The downward slope behind the claim of a negative interaction came from extreme values in two metropolitan areas (p. 181).

Failure can also go the other way. A manuscript points to one row of a regression table, a non-significant interaction term, and concludes ‘the effect does not depend on the moderator’. It shows no estimates by range. In a manuscript that uses this paper's diagnostics, failure looks different again. It reports only the p-value of the linearity Wald test and writes ‘linearity is confirmed’. The sample is small, but there is no plot of the bin estimates on top of the linear line.
