## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether a paper that rests a claim on an interaction with a continuous variable treats the Brambor, Clark, and Golder (2006) checklist as sufficient.** Including the constitutive terms and plotting marginal effects from the linear model is now routine. It cannot reveal the two problems the paper documents. One is a nonlinear interaction, which breaks the linear interaction effect (LIE) assumption that the treatment effect changes at a constant rate. The other is a lack of common support: too few observations, or no variation in the treatment, at some moderator values. Ask such a paper for raw-data plots of the outcome against the moderator, by treatment group, with linear and LOESS fits.

**(b) Check whether marginal effects are reported only where the data support them.** An effect at a given moderator value needs observations near that value and variation in the treatment there. The plot should show the moderator's distribution, split by treatment group when the treatment is binary, because a pooled histogram cannot show whether both groups are present. Outside the supported range, the estimates come from the assumed functional form, not from the data. Dropping a few extreme observations is a test of fragility, not a fix.

**(c) Check whether the paper compares the linear marginal-effect line with a flexible estimate, such as binning at the moderator's terciles or a kernel estimate, and whether the claimed interaction survives the comparison.** Bin estimates far from the line, or non-monotonic ones, are evidence against the linear model. A claim that the effect changes with the moderator should rest on a test of the difference between the effects at typical low and typical high values, not only on a significant interaction coefficient. When both variables are continuous, the diagnostics should run in both directions.

**(d) Check which way the conclusion could be wrong, given the form of the claim.** Claims about the effect at specific moderator values, such as a threshold above which the effect becomes significant, depend heavily on the linear form. When the threshold lies in a sparse tail, a flexible estimator will show wide intervals there, and the claim may not survive. The error can also run the other way. A weak linear interaction can hide a strong non-monotonic one, so a non-significant interaction term does not show that the effect is the same at all moderator values. Here the reviewer should raise the possibility, not assert hidden heterogeneity.

**(e) If a paper uses these diagnostics, check whether it treats a failure to reject linearity as proof that the linear model is correct.** The Wald test that compares the binning and linear models can be underpowered when the sample is small. Ask to see the bin estimates plotted against the linear line instead.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** After describing the Brambor, Clark, and Golder (2006) checklist as current best practice, the authors point to "two important problems that are currently often overlooked and not detected by scholars using the existing guidelines" (p. 164). The two problems are the LIE assumption (p. 164) and the lack of common support (p. 165). The first diagnostic the authors recommend is a scatterplot of the raw data for each treatment group, with linear and LOESS fits (p. 168), and it is the first item of their revised checklist (p. 187).

**For (b).** The revised checklist states: "Researchers should avoid computing marginal effects in areas where the data is too sparse either because there are no observations for those values of the moderator or there is no variation in the treatment." (p. 187). For a binary treatment, the authors recommend a histogram split by group, to show the overlap between groups (p. 172, n. 19). The second condition fails outright in two replications. In Chapman (2009), all observations with a U.N. authorization fall in the lowest tercile of the moderator (p. 178). In Clark and Golder (2006), 59% of observations have a moderator value of zero, where the treatment does not vary at all (p. 182). The authors call such estimates "model dependent and fragile" (p. 178), not biased: if the model happens to be correct, they are still consistent (p. 167, n. 11). In Malesky, Schuler, and Tran (2012), removing four leverage points, less than 0.9% of the data, flattens the estimates (p. 181). But the authors stress that they have no theoretical reason to drop these points and do so only as a robustness check (p. 181, n. 28).

**For (c).** The revised checklist makes the linear model conditional on a flexible check: "The standard linear interaction model and marginal-effect plots should only be used if the estimates from the binning and/or kernel estimator suggest that the interaction is really linear" (p. 187). Both estimators relax the LIE assumption (p. 171; p. 173). The authors also give the rule for reading the plot: "When the estimates from the binning estimator are far off the line or when they are non-monotonic, we have evidence that the LIE assumption does not hold." (p. 173). The low-versus-high test is the authors' first scoring criterion: it compares the effects at the medians of the lowest and highest terciles (p. 183). In roughly 70% of the replicated cases, it could not reject equal effects once the LIE assumption was relaxed (p. 164). Diagnostics in both directions are recommended because the standard model assumes linearity both ways (p. 187).

**For (d).** The authors observe that researchers go beyond on-average conclusions and "instead claim to have estimated the marginal effect of the treatment at specific values of the moderator, interpretations which rely heavily on the linear functional form being correct" (p. 165; the sentence begins on p. 164). Malesky, Schuler, and Tran (2012) is the threshold case. In the original model, the effect becomes significant only above an Internet penetration of about 2.5, where there are very few observations, and none of the binned estimates is significant (p. 181). Flexible estimators make this visible by widening their intervals where support is thin, which the authors call desirable (p. 175). The general direction stated in (d) is this memo's reading of these cases, not a rule the authors state. For the opposite direction, the authors' simulation with a quadratic effect (p. 168) finds that "the standard linear model indicates a slightly negative, but overall very weak, interaction effect, whereas the binning estimates reveal that the effect of D is actually strongly conditioned by X" (p. 173). This is a single simulation, and the replications include only studies that claimed an interaction (p. 175). So the paper does not show how often a null result hides heterogeneity in practice.

**For (e).** The authors test linearity with a Wald test of whether the binning model improves on the linear model. They warn that failing to reject "does not necessarily mean that the LIE assumption holds, especially when the sample size is small and the test is underpowered" (p. 183). A paper that reads a non-significant test as confirming linearity makes the inference the authors warn against.

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** papers in which a substantive claim rests on a multiplicative interaction between a treatment and a moderator, at least one of them continuous, estimated by OLS or a linear fixed-effects model. This is the setting the authors say the existing guidelines miss (p. 164) and the one they sampled (p. 175). Which variable is called the treatment does not matter (p. 166, n. 7). Check (a) applies when the checklist is the paper's only safeguard. It is silent once raw-data plots or flexible estimates are reported, and (b) and (c) then read them. Check (b) applies whenever marginal effects are reported across a wide range of the moderator, and matters most when the moderator is skewed or the treatment is concentrated in part of its range, as in Chapman (2009) and Clark and Golder (2006) (p. 178; p. 182). It is silent when effects are shown only over the supported range, with the moderator's distribution by group. The linearity question in (c) is silent when the paper already uses binning, separate estimates by moderator range, a GAM, or kernel or machine-learning methods, which the authors do not criticize and partly recommend (p. 163, n. 1; p. 187). The low-versus-high test still applies. Check (d) applies only to claims about specific moderator values, or to claims of no heterogeneity drawn from a non-significant interaction term. It is silent for claims about the average slope, which use regression as "a linear approximation for the unknown true model", a use the authors accept (p. 164); (c) covers those claims. Check (e) applies only to papers that report these diagnostics.

**Does not apply to:** designs in which both the treatment and the moderator are binary or take only a few values, such as a 2×2 factorial experiment. The authors recommend a fully saturated model there (p. 166), and a saturated model makes no LIE assumption. The checks also do not apply to mediation analysis (p. 166, n. 6) or to regressions without an interaction term, even though the authors note that misspecification and support problems arise there too (p. 165).

**Does not apply to** identification. The sharpest boundary is in the authors' conclusion: the checks "cannot help with other common problems such as endogeneity or omitted variables" (p. 188), and following them "does not guarantee that the model will be correctly specified" (p. 187). A reviewer should not use these checks to argue that the moderator is endogenous. Nor should a reviewer read a paper that passes all five checks as having a causal or correctly specified conditional effect.

**Partial application:** interactions in logit, probit, or other nonlinear models, and triple interactions. The replications used only OLS and linear fixed-effects models and excluded triple interactions because they "impose even more demanding assumptions" (p. 175). Flag the concern, but do not assert that the diagnostics apply as they are. Two smaller limits also apply. In small samples, noisy bin estimates are not proof of nonlinearity, because flexible estimators are less efficient when the linear model is right (p. 170). And when the treatment is randomized independently of the moderator, treatment variation is largely ensured by design (this memo's reading), so (b) reduces to checking thin tails. Even the authors' well-behaved case, Huddy, Mason, and Aarøe (2015), had good support only from about 0.3 to 1 on a 0-to-1 moderator (p. 176).

## 5. What failure looks like

A paper draws a substantive conclusion from an interaction with a continuous moderator and cites Brambor, Clark, and Golder (2006) as its methodological safeguard. Its evidence is one marginal-effect plot from the linear model: a straight line across the full range of the moderator, with no histogram or only a pooled one. The text then describes the effect at specific values, for example as significant only above some threshold, without saying how many treated and control observations lie beyond it. Neither the paper nor its appendix reports raw-data plots, binning or kernel estimates, or a test of the low-versus-high difference. The opposite failure reads a non-significant interaction term as proof that the effect does not depend on the moderator. And a paper that does run these diagnostics may report a non-significant linearity test in a small sample as confirming that the effect is linear.
